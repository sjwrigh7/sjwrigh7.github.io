M.S. Research

I attented Clemson University for my M.S. in civil engineering, with an emphasis in structural engineering, starting in the autumn of 2019 and graduating in August of 2020.
My first semester, I did not have an advisor and so I was only doing coursework, but I was able to find an advisor that semester, Dr. Laura Redmond. We began discussing different research topics and we eventually arrived on the topic of retrofit of concrete strctural members. Specifically, the focus was on novel column confinement methods to retrofit post-earthquake concrete columns. We were interested in on-site application of additively manufactured column jacketing. At first, we considered ultrasonic additive manufacturing, an emerging method for additive manufacturing of metals. This proved not feasible at the time due to the cost requirements of ultrasonic additive manufacturing at the time. We then turned our focus to 3D printed fiber reinforced polymers. I began looking into various methods to test the mechanical properties of different polymer matrices and the bond strength between these polymers and glass fibers as well as the bond strength between the polymers and concrete surfaces.

  • Tensile testing of cured polymer dogbone specimens
  • Polymer-fiber shear bond testing
  • Polymer-concrete shear bond testing
  • Polymer-concrete tensile-bond testing
While working on the experimental testing, I was simultaneously working on designing a prototype 3D printer specifically for printing around 4" x 8" concrete cylinders; however, at this time I realized that there were serious concerns about the curing time necessary for the better performing polymers, which could pose a problem for a 3D printing application. After some research polymer curing, I learned that some polymers can cure more quickly when heated. While this could have potentially resolved the curing time issue at hand, it also introduced more complexity to the problem, and ultimately changed the trajectory of the M.S. research. Now that heat could be used to help with curing the polymer, there was a concern about the affect that this heat might have on the mechanical properties of the concrete itself. There is a plethora of research on the long-term effects of heat on concrete compressive strength in literature, but the heating regimes anticipated for our application did not well-match those previously studied. Thus, the focus of my research shifted from the retrofit of columns to investigating the effects of heat on concrete compressive strength in non-standard heating regimes. I conducted an experimental campaing examining the percentage of concrete compressive strength loss for various exposure times and exposure temperatures of 4" x 8" concrete cylinders heated in a furnace. Next, I created a finite element model of a single concrete cylinder to solve the transient heat transfer of the cylinder in the furnace for each of the experimental test conditions. I modeled convection boundary conditions on the cylinder faces using Nusselt number correlations with a linearized radiation heat transfer term. The finite element model was calibrated to experimentally collected temperature data from thermocouples placed on the cylinder surfaces, inside the cylinders, and measuring the air temperature in the furnace. With the calibrated finite element model's transient temperature field, I devised an algorithm to aggregate cylinder's cross-sectional transient temperature over the heating and cooling regime into measures of total effective time that the cylinder was inside specific temperature ranges, with the ranges taken from existing experimental investigations of critical temperature ranges for different concrete compressive strength degredation mechanisms. From here, I used a generalized linear model to estimate relate the percentage of concrete compressive strength loss as a function of these total effective times from the algorithm, yielding strong results on the training data, and proving effective for test data from other literature.
